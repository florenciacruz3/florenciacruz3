# What I Wish Someone Had Told Me About the Tech Industry Before I Entered It 

*The unwritten rules, the assumptions, and the surprises*

The tech industry has grown insanely over the course of my lifetime.
   
I myself grew up in a household where technology was everything. My dad has been involved in the tech industry ever since I can remember. And so are all of his friends. 

So, I grew up hearing terms like Power BI, Azure, Developers, and Software Architecture, just to name a few. And I would even wear some of the merchandise that my dad would get at conferences with words on them I didn’t know what they meant. 

It all seemed very complex and impossible to get into, especially as a woman. So, I decided as a little kid, that tech was just not for me. I liked anything that involved reading and writing. And I followed that path, I got a bachelor’s degree from UW in English, creative writing. 

I didn't know where to begin when it came to finding a job that would allow me to do that. 

Four months ago, I found out that technical writing in the tech industry was a thing, and all of my beliefs and assumptions have completely changed. 

Here’s what no one ever told me before starting: 

**1.	Those terms that always seemed so scary are not.**
   
One of the first things I did when I started working with @Gaston Cruz, was writing my first real life project, The Hidden Risks in Your Microsoft Fabric Tenant – and How to Find Them Before They Find You. It sounds scary, doesn’t it? 

But, what is a Tenant Scan? And what is Microsoft Fabric? What does migration mean in this context? These were all questions that I had to ask at least a thousand times, until I finally got it. And once I got it, it didn’t seem scary anymore. It was actually pretty simple and straightforward. From then on, I was able to figure most of it out and write this article.

A good technical writer doesn't need to be an expert in tech. They don’t need to be software engineers or experts. A technical writer is someone who has the ability to turn complex concepts into simple, user-friendly content.

**2.	The audience matters more than you think.**
   
The audience means everything when it comes to technical writing. It’s about knowing what they know, what they don’t know, and what they need to know. 

Just as I did with my tenant scan article, knowing whether the audience is a software developer, a CEO, a manager, or a complete beginner changes everything — the reader might know what Microsoft Fabric is or not, they might know what a tenant scan is or not, they might not know what Power BI is, or they might not know what any of these terms mean, and that is okay.

A technical writer's job is to serve the audience they are writing for.

When I wrote my Getting Started with Markdown article, it was directed to college students with only knowledge on Microsoft Word and Google Docs, but no knowledge in Markdown or GitHub. That made a huge difference in aspects such as the voice of narration, what to include in this article, and what not to. 

**3.	Success within the tech industry doesn’t mean working in Microsoft for 30 years.**
   
My ambitions when I entered the tech industry were to take a few certifications, write a few articles, and start working in Microsoft as a technical writer. Once I did that, I wouldn’t have to worry about anything else. 

But that's not what happened. While working under the mentorship of Gastón, I did start with technical writing, but also expanded to accessibility, UX writing, information architecture, and project management. 

And life took me to different paths too. I got offered the opportunity of working for @Erik Johnson as an account executive at @Informed Decision. 

I got to learn how to manage a HubSpot CRM account, use AI in so many unexpected ways, and develop skills, such as problem-solving, taking initiative, and managing sales strategies. 

**The Outcome**

My career didn’t stop here. I had the opportunity of meeting with @Miri Rodriguez. I’ve been admiring her work all through my technical writing journey, and I got a position as an intern at @Empressa. 

Once I did enough research, I learned the differences between a career economy and a portfolio economy. Working in a single comfortable position doesn't necessarily mean stability. Having an extensive portfolio, being open to new opportunities, being able to think outside the box, and being willing to grow it’s what for me now, I would call stability. 

Before meeting with her, I had no idea what the concept of AI directed for women even meant. I didn’t know how the use of storytelling could even be applied to technical writing. 

And I couldn’t see how expanding my knowledge, or more like deviating it in my head, could even land me an opportunity like this. 

**Conclusion**

As a woman in tech, I can tell you, it is scary and intimidating at first. But once you break those barriers, you’ll find that you’re more than capable of doing it. And once it becomes easy, that just means it’s time to start expanding. 

Don’t limit yourself to one role and one company, you’re more than that.
